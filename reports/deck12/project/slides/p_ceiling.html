Takeaway: a swinging network is not a reason to adapt

Bars: the ceiling per recording · Verizon, AT&T and T-Mobile drives · capacity-only replay, not a live mobile test

09

WHAT TO SAY: Mobile networks swing wildly: on our five recorded drives, the speed of a typical fast second is 6.5 to 25 times that of a typical slow one. So, does adapting help there? Instead of building a learning controller first, we asked: what is the most any controller could gain? We computed a perfect controller that knows the future and picks the best number of connections for each 120-second stretch of each recording, paying the real cost of each switch, with the piece size fixed at 1 MiB, in our trace-replay model. Even it was only 0.41 % faster on average than simply keeping the best fixed number. We had decided in advance that adaptation would only be worth pursuing if this ceiling was at least 10 %; run-to-run noise in our campaigns was about 1 to 5 %, so under 5 % counts as too weak and 10 % as meaningful. Even the cautious lower estimate was below zero, so the answer is no. The reason: when the network gets slower, every setting gets slower together. The best number of connections stays about the same, roughly “enough to fill the pipe”. We replayed capacity only: no delay, loss or server behaviour changes, so this is a replay, not a live mobile test. IN SIMPLE WORDS: The network changing a lot does not mean there is anything to gain by changing settings. Variability is not opportunity. THE CHART: each bar is one recording; its height is the most a perfect controller could gain, in percent. The red dashed line is the 10 % bar we set in advance. TERMS ON THIS SLIDE: • Perfect controller (“oracle”): imaginary, knows the future. It gives a ceiling: no real controller that switches N can beat it. • Ceiling (“headroom”): how much faster the perfect controller is than the best fixed setting. • Cautious lower estimate (“one-sided 95 % lower bound”): the value we can be fairly sure the gain is at least. It was −0.26 %, so we cannot even be sure the gain is above zero. • Recording (“trace”): a recorded second-by-second capacity of a real mobile network while driving, replayed through a link emulator.
