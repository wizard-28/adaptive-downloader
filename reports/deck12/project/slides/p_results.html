WHAT TO SAY: The chart on the left shows adaptation happening in one real download, which is also our fallback if the live demo fails. The server’s cap switches on at 8 seconds, shown by the red dashed line. The top panel is the speed of one request: it holds near 2 MiB/s, collapses after the cap, and recovers once the controller reacts. The bottom panel shows the two dials. The drop rule notices the speed has fallen below half its recent best, about a second and a half after the cap, and cuts N from 16 to 4 in a single decision. Then it raises S, the piece size, one step at a time from 1 to 4 MiB. N and S are never changed in the same decision. Over ten paired runs this took 58.7 seconds on average, against 76.7 seconds for the best fixed setting we tried. The four numbers on the right are the results to remember; the next two slides show the mobile-trace and engine results in detail. 62.4 %: on a server that starts slow and then speeds up, our downloader on one fixed setting took 62.4 % less time than aria2. We explain most of that as a timing effect: its requests start after the speed-up, whereas aria2 starts its big pieces while the server is slow. It is not adaptation. 0.41 %: we replayed five recorded mobile networks, whose speed swings by 6.5 to 25 times. Even a perfect controller that knows the future could save only 0.41 % on average, so there was almost nothing to gain. 1.071: under a server that throttles when you open too many connections, our simple drop rule, once we fixed two engine weaknesses, was 7 % slower than the best fixed setting. 0.907: a learning agent retrained on that throttling server was 9 % faster than the fixed 1 MiB setting there. With broader training, an agent also won on 3 of 4 new conditions inside its training ranges, but on none outside them, and on three real public mirrors our adaptive controllers failed the no-harm test. For testing: our automated tests cover every part of the code, the race detector is clean, every downloaded file is checked byte for byte, and a script recomputes all 231 numbers in our paper from the raw data. SETTINGS BEHIND THE NUMBERS (if asked): 62.4 % is on the server that starts slow and then speeds up, our fixed 16 connections of 4 MiB against aria2. 0.41 % is for a perfect controller that can only switch N, between 120-second stretches of each recorded trace, with S fixed at 1 MiB, in our trace-replay model. 1.071 is the drop rule against the best fixed setting we ran, 16 connections of 32 MiB, with the cap from 8 s and the fixed engine; the paper calls this controller the causal controller. The 58.7 s in the timeline is against the best of the three fixed settings we tried under that schedule. 0.907 is the retrained agent against fixed 16 connections of 1 MiB on the server it was trained for. We tested delay and loss only at fixed levels, never changing during a download. Why a 10 % bar for the perfect controller: run-to-run noise in our campaigns was about 1 to 5 %, so under 5 % counted as too weak and 10 % as meaningful. IN SIMPLE WORDS: Adapting almost never beat a well-chosen fixed setting. The big wins came from how the downloader is built, not from clever control. TERMS ON THIS SLIDE: • Time ratio: our time divided by the other option’s time. 0.80 means 20 % less time; 1.25 means 25 % slower. • Perfect controller (“oracle”) and headroom: the most any controller could gain. • Throttling server: a server that slows everyone down when too many connections are open. • Drop rule: switches once to fewer connections when the speed halves. • No-harm test: an adaptive controller passes if it is at most 5 % slower than the best fixed setting. • Race detector: Go’s tool that finds bugs when many things run at once.
