WHAT TO SAY: To fix the generalisation problem, we trained a new agent on a much wider range of conditions: weak and strong caps, short and long network delays, and sudden slow-downs. We wrote the test plan first and had it reviewed before running anything. Then we tested it on four new conditions it had never seen, all within the range it trained on: a weaker cap of 12 MiB/s, a long 110 ms delay, a cap plus a sudden dip, and a weak 20 MiB/s cap with a short delay. It was faster than the best fixed 1 MiB setting in three of them (12 %, 18 % and 5 % less time) and slightly slower, by 0.7 %, in the fourth. It beat the first retrained agent in all six tests. With no cap at all it cost about 5 %. Be honest about the limits: we trained five versions from different random starts and picked the best on practice tests; the others were worse. And the simple drop rule beat it on the scenario the rule was tuned for. IN SIMPLE WORDS: Broader practice made the agent handle new situations partly, but only situations similar to its practice, and a simple rule was still competitive. THE CHART: each group of bars is one condition. Grey = first retrained agent, blue = broadly trained agent, orange = drop rule. Bars below the line at 1 were faster than the best fixed setting. TERMS ON THIS SLIDE: • Unseen (“held-out”) conditions: test cases kept out of training. • Random start (“seed”): training from different starting points gives different agents. • Practice tests (“validation”): used to choose among the five trained versions, separate from the final tests.
